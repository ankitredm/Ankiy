The Gift of Athena

In the city of Athens, the people wished to name their city after a god or
goddess who would bestow upon them the most valuable gift. Two mighty beings
contended for the honor: Poseidon, the god of the sea, and Athena, the goddess
of wisdom and craft.

Poseidon struck the ground with his mighty trident, and there sprang forth a
magnificent horse, a creature of strength and beauty, which would serve in war
and in travel. The people marveled at the gift, for the horse was a noble and
fearless animal.

Then Athena knelt and planted a small seed in the earth. From that seed grew
an olive tree, with silver leaves and branches heavy with fruit. Its leaves
brought shade, its fruit brought food and oil for cooking and for light, and
its wood was strong and useful. The olive could not fight, but it could
sustain life and bring peace.

The people considered both gifts and chose the olive tree of Athena, for it
served the city in times of peace as well as war. And so the city was named
Athens in her honor, and she became its guardian, protector of wisdom and
learning.

The Golden Touch of King Midas

King Midas was a rich and greedy monarch who loved gold above all things. One
day, having done a kindness to the god Dionysus, the god offered to grant him
any wish. Midas did not hesitate: he wished that everything he touched could
turn to gold.

At first the gift delighted him. He touched a twig and it became gold; he
touched a stone and it glittered. But soon his joy turned to horror. When he
sat down to eat, the bread became gold in his hand, and the wine turned to
gold upon his lips. He could not eat, drink, or sleep.

Then his beloved daughter ran to him, and when he embraced her, she too was
turned into a cold and motionless statue of gold. The king wept in despair,
realizing that his greedy wish had robbed him of everything that truly
mattered.

He begged the god to take back the gift, and the god told him to wash in the
river Pactolus. When Midas plunged into the river, the golden touch flowed away
from him, and the sand of the river turned to gold from then on. Midas learned
that the love of gold above all else brings grief, and that some things are
far more precious than riches.

The Flight of Icarus

Daedalus was a master craftsman who built the great labyrinth on Crete, but he
and his son Icarus were imprisoned in a tower by the King. Longing to escape,
Daedalus fashioned wings of feathers and wax, so that they could fly away over
the sea.

Before they left, Daedalus warned his son not to fly too close to the sun, for
the heat would melt the wax, and not too close to the sea, for the spray would
weigh down the feathers. But young Icarus, exhilarated by the joy of flight,
forgot his father's warning. He soared higher and higher, drawing ever closer
to the blazing sun.

The wax softened, the feathers loosened, and Icarus fell from the sky into the
sea. Daedalus cried out in grief, but he could not save his son. And so the
story reminds us to heed wise counsel and to seek the middle path, avoiding
both the recklessness of too much ambition and the carelessness that comes from
forgetting the warnings of those who know better.
